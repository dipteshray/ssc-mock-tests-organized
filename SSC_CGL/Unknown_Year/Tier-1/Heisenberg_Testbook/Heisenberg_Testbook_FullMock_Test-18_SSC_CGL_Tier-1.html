34. Which type of spherical mirror is used by dentists to see a large image of the teeth of patients?

A. Plane mirror

B. Convex mirror

C. Concave mirror

D. Bifocal mirror

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Concave mirror Key Points Additional Information

• A Concave mirror is the type of spherical mirror used by dentists to see a large (magnified) image of a patient's teeth.

• The core logic lies in the image formation properties of a concave mirror: when an object is placed very close to the mirror (between the pole and the principal focus), the resulting image is virtual, erect, and magnified.

• Plane mirrors are incorrect because they produce an image that is the same size as the object, providing no magnification for detailed dental work.

• Convex mirrors are incorrect because they always produce a diminished (smaller) image, which would make it harder for a dentist to see details.

• Bifocal mirrors is not a standard term in optics; bifocal lenses are used in spectacles to correct vision, not for magnification in dental tools.

• Concave mirrors are also widely used in shaving mirrors and makeup mirrors to provide a clear, enlarged view of the face.

• They are used in torches, searchlights, and vehicle headlights to produce a powerful parallel beam of light when the light source is placed at the focus.

• Convex mirrors are commonly used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles because they give a wider field of view, allowing drivers to see a large area of traffic behind them.

• A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is curved inwards (towards the center of the sphere) is a concave mirror, while one curved outwards is a convex mirror.

45. "Eutrophication" in aquatic ecosystems like Kerala's backwaters is primarily driven by an excess of which nutrients?

A. Mercury and Lead

B. Nitrogen and Phosphorus

C. Carbon Dioxide and Oxygen

D. Sodium and Calcium

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Nitrogen and Phosphorus. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Eutrophication is a process where a body of water becomes overly enriched with minerals and nutrients that induce excessive growth of algae.

• This phenomenon is primarily driven by the accumulation of Nitrogen and Phosphorus in the aquatic ecosystem. These nutrients act as fertilizers for aquatic plants and microscopic organisms. Hence, the statement regarding Nitrogen and Phosphorus is correct.

• The presence of these nutrients leads to Algal Blooms, which create a dense layer on the water surface, preventing sunlight from penetrating deeper layers of the water body.

• As the algae die, microorganisms like bacteria decompose them, a process that consumes large quantities of Dissolved Oxygen (DO). This leads to a state of hypoxia, where oxygen levels are too low to support fish and other aquatic life.

• Sources of Nutrients: In India, particularly in Kerala's backwaters, the high concentration of Nitrogen and Phosphorus is attributed to agricultural runoff from the Kuttanad region, where chemical fertilizers are heavily used in paddy cultivation.

• Types of Eutrophication: Natural Eutrophication: A slow, geological process where nutrients accumulate over centuries as a lake ages.

• Cultural Eutrophication: A rapid, human-induced process caused by urbanization, industrial waste, and domestic sewage.

• Impact on Kerala: The Vembanad Lake, a designated Ramsar Site in Kerala, has suffered significantly from eutrophication, leading to the proliferation of water hyacinths and a decline in traditional shrimp and fish farming.

• Consequences: Beyond hypoxia, eutrophication can lead to the production of toxins by certain algae (cyanobacteria), which can contaminate drinking water and harm public health.

• Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD): This is a crucial numerical value used to measure water pollution. During eutrophication, the BOD increases significantly because more oxygen is needed by aerobic bacteria to break down the excess organic matter.

• Limiting Nutrients: In most freshwater ecosystems, Phosphorus is the primary limiting nutrient, meaning its addition has the most immediate impact on algal growth. In marine or brackish waters, Nitrogen often plays this role.

• Regulatory Framework: The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) in India monitors nutrient levels under the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, to mitigate the degradation of sensitive aquatic ecosystems.

• Prevention: Methods to control eutrophication include the diversion of sewage, reducing the use of phosphate-based detergents, and creating riparian buffer zones to filter agricultural runoff before it reaches the water.
