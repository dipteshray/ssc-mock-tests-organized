1. All eukaryotic cells are supported structurally by an internal network of proteins known as:

A. cytoskeleton

B. nucleus

C. mitochondria

D. ribosomes

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is cytoskeleton. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The cytoskeleton is an internal network of protein filaments and tubules present in all eukaryotic cells.

• It provides structural support to the cell, maintaining its shape and organization.

• The cytoskeleton is composed of three primary components: microfilaments (actin filaments), microtubules, and intermediate filaments.

• This network is involved in various cellular processes, including intracellular transport, cell division (mitosis and meiosis), and cell signaling.

• The cytoskeleton plays a crucial role in cell motility, enabling cells to move and change shape during processes like wound healing and immune responses.

• Components of the Cytoskeleton: Microfilaments: These are composed of actin proteins. They are involved in maintaining the cell's shape, enabling cell motility, and facilitating intracellular transport.

• Microtubules: Made up of tubulin, they provide structural support and serve as tracks for the movement of organelles and vesicles. They are also essential in forming the mitotic spindle during cell division.

• Intermediate Filaments: These filaments provide mechanical strength to cells, helping them withstand tension. Examples include keratin in skin cells.

• Functions of the Cytoskeleton: Maintains the overall cell structure and integrity.

• Assists in cellular movements such as crawling, contraction, and division.

• Plays a role in intracellular transport of organelles and molecules using motor proteins like kinesin and dynein.

• Facilitates communication between the cell surface and its internal organelles.

• Comparison with Other Organelles: Nucleus: It is the control center of the cell, housing DNA and regulating gene expression. However, it does not provide structural support like the cytoskeleton.

• Mitochondria: Known as the powerhouse of the cell, it generates energy in the form of ATP but has no role in structural support.

• Ribosomes: These are involved in protein synthesis and are not connected to structural functions.

• Applications and Research: Understanding the cytoskeleton is critical in studying diseases such as cancer, as changes in cell shape and motility are hallmarks of metastasis.

• Cytoskeletal proteins are targeted in therapies for neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's disease.

• Research into cytoskeletal dynamics has applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

• The cytoskeleton is dynamic, meaning it can rapidly reorganize to adapt to the cell's needs, such as during cell division or migration.

• It interacts with motor proteins like myosin, kinesin, and dynein, which help in the movement of vesicles and organelles within the cell.

• Mutations in cytoskeletal proteins can lead to diseases such as cardiomyopathy, muscular dystrophy, and neurodegenerative disorders.

• Cytoskeleton research has greatly advanced with the development of advanced microscopy techniques like fluorescent and electron microscopy.

2. What is the boiling point of water at sea level?

A. 208.4 °F

B. 217.4 °F

C. 212 °F

D. 203 °F

Answer C

Solution

The Correct answer is 212 ºF Key Points Additional Information

• The boiling point of water at sea level is 212 ºF, which is equivalent to 100 ºC.

• This boiling point is determined under standard atmospheric pressure, which is approximately 1 atmosphere (101.325 kPa).

• At this temperature, water transitions from the liquid phase to the gas phase, forming steam.

• The boiling point of water can change with variations in altitude and atmospheric pressure. For example, at higher altitudes, where the atmospheric pressure is lower, water boils at a temperature below 212 ºF.

• Boiling water is crucial in various scientific, industrial, and daily applications, such as cooking, sterilization, and generating steam for energy production.

• The boiling point of water is often used as a reference point in thermometry and other scientific measurements.

• Altitude and Boiling Point Relationship As altitude increases, the atmospheric pressure decreases, which reduces the boiling point of water.

• For example, at an altitude of around 5,000 feet, water boils at approximately 203 ºF.

• This principle is significant in mountainous regions and affects cooking times for food preparation.

• Applications of Boiling Water Boiling water is widely used for purification and sterilization to kill harmful microbes.

• It is a key component in steam engines, which were historically significant in industrial development.

• Boiling water plays an essential role in scientific experiments and chemical processes.

5. The magnitude of buoyant force depends on the _________ of the fluid.

A. refractive index

B. density

C. colour

D. charge

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is density. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Buoyant force is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an object submerged in it. This force enables objects to float or rise in fluids.

• The magnitude of buoyant force depends on the density of the fluid. Higher fluid density results in a greater buoyant force.

• According to Archimedes' principle, the buoyant force is equal to the weight of the displaced fluid. The weight of the displaced fluid is determined by its density and volume.

• The formula for buoyant force is: Fb = ρ × V × g, where ρ is the density of the fluid, V is the volume of displaced fluid, and g is the acceleration due to gravity.

• Other properties like refractive index, colour, or charge do not influence buoyant force. These are unrelated to the physical interaction between the object and fluid.

• Archimedes' Principle: It states that any object fully or partially submerged in a fluid experiences a buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid.

• This principle is applicable to both liquids and gases.

• It was discovered by the Greek mathematician and inventor Archimedes around 250 BC.

• Density: Density is defined as mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V.

• Units of density are typically expressed in kg/m³ or g/cm³.

• Examples: Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³, while air has a density of approximately 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level.

• Applications of Buoyant Force: Shipbuilding: Large ships float because their design ensures the weight of displaced water is greater than the weight of the ship.

• Submarines: Submarines adjust their buoyancy by controlling water and air in ballast tanks.

• Hot Air Balloons: The buoyant force from the surrounding cooler air lifts the balloon filled with heated air.

• Factors Affecting Buoyant Force: Density of Fluid: Higher density fluids exert greater buoyant force.

• Volume of Object Submerged: Larger submerged volume displaces more fluid and increases buoyant force.

• Gravitational Acceleration: Variations in gravity influence buoyant force magnitude (e.g., weaker gravity on the Moon).

• Refractive Index: This property measures how much light bends when it enters a material. It has no connection to buoyant force.

• Colour: Colour is a visual property determined by the wavelength of light reflected by an object or fluid. It does not affect buoyancy.

• Charge: Charge refers to the electrical property of particles. It does not interact with buoyant force, which is a mechanical phenomenon.

6. Which of the following phenomena occurs when a wave passes through a narrow slit?

A. Refraction

B. Reflection

C. Absorption

D. Diffraction

Answer D

Solution

The correct answer is Diffraction. Key Points Additional Information

• Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or the spreading of waves as they pass through narrow slits or openings.

• The phenomenon is more pronounced when the size of the slit or obstacle is comparable to the wavelength of the wave.

• Diffraction occurs for all types of waves, including light waves, sound waves, and water waves.

• This bending allows waves to propagate into the geometrical shadow region, creating interference patterns.

• In optics, diffraction effects are essential in understanding the behavior of light in devices such as diffraction gratings and optical instruments.

• Wavelength: The distance between consecutive crests or troughs of a wave. Diffraction depends on the wavelength relative to the size of the slit or obstacle.

• Interference Patterns: Diffraction often produces constructive and destructive interference patterns, leading to regions of bright and dark fringes.

• Huygens' Principle: A foundational principle in wave optics stating that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets, which combine to form the observed wave.

• Applications of Diffraction: Used in X-ray crystallography to determine atomic structures, and in spectrometers and diffraction gratings for analyzing light spectra.

• Double-Slit Experiment: A classic demonstration of diffraction and interference, highlighting the wave nature of light and other particles.

7. What type of radiation is responsible for ionizing atmospheric gases in the Earth's ionosphere?

A. Cosmic Radiation

B. Solar Radiation

C. Ultraviolet Radiation

D. Gamma Radiation

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Ultraviolet Radiation Key Points Additional Information

• The ionosphere is a region of the Earth's upper atmosphere, extending from approximately 60 km to 1,000 km above the surface, where the air is ionized by solar energy.

• Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun is the primary agent responsible for this ionization. UV photons possess sufficient energy to knock electrons out of the atoms and molecules of atmospheric gases (such as oxygen and nitrogen), creating a layer of free electrons and positive ions.

• While Solar Radiation is the broad term for all energy emitted by the Sun, Ultraviolet Radiation (along with X-rays) is the specific component of the electromagnetic spectrum that drives the ionization process in the upper atmosphere.

• Cosmic Radiation, which originates from outside the solar system, does contribute to ionization, but its effect is significantly smaller than that of solar UV radiation within the ionosphere.

• Gamma Radiation is highly energetic but is not the primary source for the sustained ionization layers found in the Earth's ionosphere.

• The ionosphere is critical for radio communication because it reflects high-frequency (HF) or shortwave radio waves back to Earth, allowing for long-distance transmission beyond the horizon.

• It is composed of several layers, known as the D, E, and F layers. The F layer (also known as the Appleton-Barnett layer) has the highest electron density and is most important for skywave propagation.

• Ionization levels fluctuate based on the diurnal cycle (day and night), seasons, and the 11-year solar cycle. During the night, the D and E layers largely disappear as ions and electrons recombine in the absence of solar UV.

• Phenomena such as the Aurora Borealis (Northern Lights) occur when charged particles from the solar wind interact with the gases in the ionosphere near the Earth's magnetic poles.

8. Why is it more difficult to walk on sand than on a concrete pavement?

A. Sand has a higher coefficient of static friction than concrete.

B. Sand is less dense than concrete, causing less gravitational pull.

C. Sand displaces, reducing pushback

D. Sand particles are magnetic, creating an opposing force.

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Sand displaces, reducing pushback. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Sand displaces under pressure: When you walk on sand, the particles beneath your feet shift and move apart. This displacement reduces the ability of the sand to provide a firm surface for pushback, making walking more difficult. Hence, this explains the correct answer.

• Concrete provides a rigid surface: Unlike sand, concrete is a solid, immovable surface. It does not shift or deform under pressure, allowing for greater pushback and making walking easier.

• Lack of resistance on sand: Sand does not offer as much resistance as concrete because it is composed of loose particles that can easily rearrange. This lack of resistance makes walking on sand more effort-intensive.

• Energy dissipation on sand: When you walk on sand, much of the energy you exert is absorbed by the shifting particles. This energy dissipation requires you to exert more force to propel yourself forward.

• Weight distribution: On sand, your weight causes localized displacement of particles, leading to instability and a need for constant adjustments in your gait. This does not occur on a solid surface like concrete.

• Coefficient of static friction: The coefficient of static friction is the measure of how much frictional force exists between two surfaces before motion begins.

• Sand typically has a lower coefficient of static friction compared to concrete, meaning it provides less grip. This makes walking on sand less stable compared to concrete.

• Density of sand vs. concrete: Sand is less dense than concrete, but this has no direct impact on gravitational pull. Gravitational pull depends on the mass of the Earth and the individual walking, not the density of the surface.

• Magnetic properties of sand: Sand particles are not inherently magnetic under normal conditions. Hence, there is no opposing magnetic force affecting your ability to walk on sand.

• Energy efficiency on different surfaces: Walking on sand requires 2–2.5 times more energy compared to walking on a hard surface like concrete due to the loose nature of sand and its energy-absorbing properties.

• Practical examples: Walking along a beach is more tiring than walking on a sidewalk due to the reasons outlined above.

• Sports like beach volleyball are more challenging than their court-based counterparts due to the added effort required to move on sand.

• Concrete pavements: Concrete pavements are designed to offer high stability, firmness, and resistance to pressure. This makes them easier to walk on compared to natural, loose surfaces like sand.

• Energy transfer and mechanics: The mechanics of walking involve transferring energy to the surface for pushback. On sand, this energy is absorbed and dissipated due to particle displacement, while on concrete, it is efficiently transferred back for forward motion.

• Biomechanics of walking: Walking on sand requires adjustments in stride length and gait to maintain balance, leading to increased muscular effort and fatigue.

12. A car accelerates uniformly from rest to a speed of 20 m/s in 5 seconds. What is the magnitude of its acceleration?

A. 2 m/s2

B. 4 m/s2

C. 10 m/s2

D. 25 m/s2

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is 4 m/s2 Key Points Additional Information

• Acceleration is defined as the rate of change of velocity of an object with respect to time.

• The formula to calculate uniform acceleration is derived from the first equation of motion: v = u + at, which can be rearranged as a = (v - u) / t.

• In this scenario, the car starts from rest, which means the initial velocity (u) is 0 m/s.

• The final velocity (v) reached by the car is 20 m/s, and the time (t) taken to reach this speed is 5 seconds.

• Plugging these values into the formula: a = (20 - 0) / 5.

• The calculation results in an acceleration of 4 m/s2.

• Option (1) is incorrect as 20 / 5 is not 2. Option (3) and (4) are incorrect as they represent values derived from incorrect mathematical operations (like 20 / 2 or 5 squared).

• The SI unit of acceleration is meters per second squared (m/s2).

• Acceleration is a vector quantity, meaning it possesses both magnitude and direction.

• If an object is slowing down, the acceleration is opposite to the direction of motion, often referred to as retardation or deceleration.

• Uniform acceleration implies that the velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, which is represented by a straight line on a velocity-time graph.

• The slope of a velocity-time graph gives the magnitude of acceleration.

14. Which of the following scenarios best illustrates the principle of conservation of momentum?

A. A ball rolling down a frictionless incline increases its speed.

B. A car comes to a stop when the brakes are applied due to friction.

C. When a bullet is fired from a gun, the gun recoils in the opposite direction.

D. A satellite maintains a stable orbit around the Earth due to gravity.

Answer C

Solution

When a bullet is fired from a gun, the gun recoils in the opposite direction, demonstrating the principle of conservation of momentum. The initial momentum of the system (gun + bullet) is zero. Upon firing, the forward momentum of the bullet is balanced by the backward recoil momentum of the gun, maintaining the total momentum of the system at zero. Other scenarios involve external forces like gravity or friction, which invalidate the conservation of momentum within the system.

17. The 'ossification test' is used to determine the__________. of a person.

A. Age

B. Height

C. Weight

D. Blood group

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is Age. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The ossification test is a scientific method used to determine the age of a person by studying the development and fusion of bones.

• This test is based on the principle that the human skeleton undergoes predictable changes as a person grows older, with certain bones fusing at specific ages.

• The process of ossification, or bone formation, begins in the fetus and continues into early adulthood, making it a reliable marker for age estimation.

• It is commonly used in forensic investigations, legal disputes (such as determining the age of juveniles), and in fields like anthropology and archaeology.

• The test typically involves X-ray imaging of bones like the clavicle, pelvis, wrists, and long bones, as these are key indicators of age-specific bone fusion patterns.

• Stages of Ossification: Primary ossification: This process begins during the fetal stage and involves the formation of the bone's diaphysis (shaft).

• Secondary ossification: This occurs after birth and includes the development of the epiphysis (ends of long bones).

• The fusion of epiphyseal plates (growth plates) occurs at specific ages, providing key indicators for age estimation.

• Applications of Ossification Test: Forensic Science: Used to determine the age of unidentified bodies or suspects in legal cases.

• Sports: Helps verify the age of athletes in age-restricted competitions.

• Legal Disputes: Often employed in cases involving child labor, juvenile crime, and adoption to ascertain the true age of individuals.

• Accuracy: The ossification test is generally accurate but may vary slightly depending on factors like genetics, nutrition, and environmental conditions.

• In some cases, advanced imaging techniques like CT scans and MRI are used to enhance accuracy.

• Limitations: It is less effective in adults as most bones are fully ossified by the age of 25.

• Factors such as malnutrition, hormonal disorders, and genetic anomalies can affect the ossification process, leading to discrepancies in age determination.

• Legal Use in India: In India, the ossification test is often used in court cases to determine the age of juveniles under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015.

• It is also used in cases of child labor to verify compliance with the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986.

• Bone Fusion Timelines: The clavicle is one of the last bones to ossify, completing its fusion around the age of 25.

• In children, the wrists and knees are key indicators of bone age, as they show early ossification patterns.

• Global Relevance: The ossification test is recognized worldwide for its utility in forensic and legal contexts.

• Different countries may have specific guidelines or protocols for conducting the test, ensuring its reliability and validity in various contexts.

• Sponges lack tissues and organs but have a unique cellular organization that supports their filter-feeding mechanism.

• Choanocytes: These are flagellated cells with a collar-like structure that helps in trapping food particles.

• Choanocytes are essential for the sponge's filter-feeding and water circulation.

• Water Canal System: It consists of pathways like ostia, spongocoel, and osculum through which water flows.

• The system aids in nutrient absorption, gas exchange, and waste removal.

• Skeleton of Sponges: Sponges possess a skeleton made of spicules (calcium carbonate or silica) or spongin fibers.

• Reproduction in Sponges: Sponges reproduce sexually via gametes and asexually via budding or gemmules.

• They are mostly hermaphroditic, with both male and female reproductive cells present in the same organism.

22. Under which layer of the Earth's atmosphere is the ozone layer found, which protects us from harmful ultraviolet rays?

A. Troposphere

B. Stratosphere

C. Mesosphere

D. Exosphere

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Stratosphere Key Points Additional Information

• The ozone layer or ozone shield is a region of Earth's stratosphere that absorbs most of the Sun's ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

• It contains high concentrations of ozone (O3) relative to other parts of the atmosphere, although still small relative to other gases in the stratosphere.

• The stratosphere is the second major layer of Earth's atmosphere, just above the troposphere, and below the mesosphere.

• The Troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere where humans live and where most weather occurs; it contains only about 10% of atmospheric ozone.

• The Mesosphere is the layer above the stratosphere where temperatures decrease with height and most meteors burn up upon entry.

• The Exosphere is the outermost layer of the atmosphere, thinning out until it merges with interplanetary space.

• The ozone layer was discovered in 1913 by the French physicists Charles Fabry and Henri Buisson.

• The concentration of the ozone layer is measured in Dobson Units (DU).

• The Montreal Protocol is an international treaty signed in 1987 designed to protect the ozone layer by phasing out the production of numerous substances that are responsible for ozone depletion, such as Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs).

• World Ozone Day is observed every year on September 16 to spread awareness about the depletion of the Ozone Layer and search for solutions to preserve it.

23. Which technology is increasingly being used for real-time Air Quality Index (AQI) monitoring in urban areas?

A. Remote sensing and satellite data

B. Seismic sensors

C. Sonar technology

D. Ground penetrating radar

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is Remote sensing and satellite data Key Points Additional Information

• Remote sensing and satellite data have become pivotal in monitoring the Air Quality Index (AQI) because they provide extensive spatial coverage that ground-based stations cannot achieve alone.

• Satellites equipped with advanced sensors can detect various atmospheric constituents such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and aerosols (particulate matter).

• This technology allows for real-time monitoring and the tracking of long-range transport of pollutants across borders and oceans, which is essential for urban planning and public health warnings.

• Seismic sensors are used to detect and record earthquakes and ground vibrations; they have no application in measuring air chemistry.

• Sonar technology (Sound Navigation and Ranging) is primarily used for underwater exploration, navigation, and communication.

• Ground penetrating radar (GPR) is a geophysical method used to image subsurface structures and is commonly used in archaeology and engineering, not for atmospheric analysis.

• The Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite, launched by the European Space Agency (ESA), carries the TROPOMI instrument, which is specifically designed for high-resolution global air quality monitoring.

• In India, the National Air Quality Index (AQI) was launched in October 2014 to disseminate information on air quality in an easily understandable format for the general public.

• The AQI considers eight major pollutants: PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, O3 (Ozone), NH3 (Ammonia), and Pb (Lead).

• Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) is a critical parameter measured by satellites like MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) to estimate ground-level PM2.5 concentrations.

24. What are the products in a reaction where an acid reacts with a base?

A. Acid and water

B. Base and water

C. Salt and water

D. Gas and water

Answer C

Solution

When an acid reacts with a base, it undergoes a neutralization reaction to produce salt and water. The hydrogen ions (H+) from the acid combine with hydroxide ions (OH-) from the base to form water, while the remaining ions form an ionic salt.
