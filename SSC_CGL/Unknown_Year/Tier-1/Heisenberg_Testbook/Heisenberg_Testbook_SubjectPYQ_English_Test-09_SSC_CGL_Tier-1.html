D. Galileo Galilei

Solution

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Option 3. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The body’s weight does not shift entirely to the left foot while the right leg stabilizes the upper body lean. The weight distribution varies throughout the phases, ensuring proper momentum and balance during the throw. Hence, statement 4 is incorrect.

• The correct biomechanical technique involves bending the right knee after forward extension to maintain balance and maximize the throw’s efficiency. This technique is crucial for proper stabilization. Hence, statement 3 is correct.

• The transition phase involves shifting the weight between the feet to prepare for rotational acceleration.

• The delivery phase is where the discus is released, requiring precise coordination and balance.

• The follow-through phase ensures that the thrower maintains balance and avoids injury.

• Key Techniques in Discus Throw: The thrower should focus on maintaining a low center of gravity for stability during rotational movements.

5. The first peptide to be produced after wounding in the tomato plant is ___________.

A. Systemin

B. Ricin

C. Jasmonic acid

D. Saicyclic acid

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is Systemin. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Systemin is an 18-amino acid peptide that acts as a primary signaling molecule in tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum) and other members of the Solanaceae family. It was the first peptide hormone ever discovered in plants, identified in 1991 by the research team led by Clarence A. Ryan.

• The peptide is released from a much larger precursor protein called prosystemin, which contains 200 amino acids. Upon wounding caused by herbivorous insects or mechanical damage, prosystemin is proteolytically processed to release the active systemin.

• Its primary function is to mediate the systemic wound response. Once released at the site of injury, it travels through the phloem to distant leaves, acting as a long-distance signal to prepare the entire plant for potential further attack.

• In the target cells, Systemin binds to a cell surface receptor (SR160, a member of the Leucine-Rich Repeat Receptor-Like Kinase family). This binding triggers a signaling cascade that activates the octadecanoid pathway, eventually leading to the synthesis of Jasmonic Acid.

• The ultimate result of this signaling is the induction of defense genes, most notably those encoding Protease Inhibitors (PIs). These inhibitors interfere with the digestive enzymes of insects, making the plant tissues indigestible and deterring further feeding.

• Jasmonic Acid (JA): Jasmonic acid is a lipid-derived hormone synthesized from linolenic acid via the octadecanoid pathway. While it is crucial for wounding defense, it is a small molecule hormone, not a peptide. It works downstream of Systemin to activate the transcription of defense proteins.

• Salicylic Acid (SA): Salicylic acid is a phenolic phytohormone primarily involved in Systemic Acquired Resistance (SAR). It plays a major role in defending the plant against biotrophic pathogens (which feed on living tissue). Unlike systemin, which responds to chewing insects, SA is the lead signal for microbial infections.

• Ricin: Ricin is a highly potent toxin found in the seeds of the Castor oil plant (Ricinus communis). It is a Type II ribosome-inactivating protein (RIP) that inhibits protein synthesis in eukaryotic cells. It is not a signaling peptide involved in tomato plant wounding but is a chemical defense against ingestion.

• Ethylene: Ethylene is a gaseous plant hormone that often works in synergy with Jasmonic acid during the wound response. It is involved in fruit ripening, senescence, and abscission, as well as reacting to necrotrophic pathogens.

• The discovery of Systemin challenged the long-held belief that plant hormones were only small molecules like auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins. It established that peptides could also serve as potent long-distance messengers in plants.

• Phloem transport: The ability of Systemin to move through the sieve tubes of the phloem allows the plant to coordinate a whole-body defense within hours of an localized attack.

• Specificity: While Systemin is specific to Solanaceous plants, other plant families use different Peptide Elucidators (Peps) to achieve similar immune responses.

7. What is another use of bleaching powder besides bleaching?

A. Making soap

B. Disinfecting drinking water

C. Making detergent

D. Making paint

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Disinfecting drinking water Key Points Additional Information

• Bleaching powder, chemically known as Calcium Oxychloride (CaOCl_2), is extensively used as a disinfectant for drinking water to make it free from germs and pathogens.

• When bleaching powder is added to water, it releases chlorine, which acts as a powerful germicide that kills bacteria and other microorganisms, ensuring the water is safe for consumption.

• Making soap and making detergent primarily involve the process of saponification, which uses fats or oils and strong alkalis like Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) or Potassium Hydroxide (KOH), rather than bleaching powder.

• Making paint involves pigments, binders, and solvents; bleaching powder is not a standard ingredient in the manufacturing of paint.

• Bleaching powder is produced by the action of chlorine gas on dry slaked lime [Ca(OH)_2].

• It is used as a strong oxidizing agent in many chemical industries due to its ability to release oxygen.

• In the textile industry, it is used for bleaching cotton and linen, and in the paper industry, it is used for bleaching wood pulp.

• It is also used for bleaching washed clothes in laundries and for the unshrinkable treatment of wool.

• Bleaching powder should be stored in a cool, dry place because it loses its chlorine content when exposed to air or moisture.

9. Which of the following statements is correct with respect to dimensional analysis?

A. An equation that is dimensionally consistent is always physically correct.

B. Dimensional analysis can verify both the magnitude and the physical validity of an equation.

C. Trigonometric and logarithmic functions are valid only when applied to quantities that are dimensionless.

D. Refractive index has dimensions because it involves the speed of light.

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Trigonometric and logarithmic functions are valid only when applied to quantities that are dimensionless. Key Points Additional Information

• Trigonometric, logarithmic, and exponential functions are mathematical operations that must take dimensionless arguments. For example, in the expression (θ), the angle θ must be dimensionless. Similarly, in (x) or e^x, x must be a pure number.

• An equation that is dimensionally consistent is not necessarily physically correct. For instance, the equation s = ut + at^2 is dimensionally consistent (all terms have dimensions of length), but it is physically incorrect because the correct kinematic formula is s = ut + (1/2)at^2.

• Dimensional analysis cannot verify the magnitude or dimensionless constants in an equation. It only checks if the units on both sides of the equation are compatible.

• The Refractive Index is defined as the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in a medium (n = c/v). Since it is a ratio of two similar physical quantities (speeds), it is a dimensionless quantity.

• The Principle of Homogeneity of Dimensions states that the dimensions of all the terms in a physical expression must be the same.

• Other common dimensionless quantities include strain, relative density, solid angle, and plane angle.

• While dimensional analysis is a powerful tool for checking the consistency of equations and deriving relations, its main limitation is its inability to determine proportionality constants or handle summation/difference of terms that are not of the same dimension.

10. What is the name of the layer of the Earth's atmosphere where weather phenomena occur?

A. Stratosphere

B. Mesosphere

C. Troposphere

D. Thermosphere

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Troposphere Key Points Additional Information

• The Troposphere is the lowest layer of the Earth's atmosphere and is the region where we live. It contains approximately 75% to 80% of the total mass of the atmosphere.

• Almost all weather phenomena, including cloud formation, rain, snow, and storms, occur in this layer because it contains nearly all of the atmosphere's water vapor and aerosols.

• In the troposphere, the temperature generally decreases with altitude at a rate known as the environmental lapse rate.

• The Stratosphere is the layer above the troposphere; it contains the Ozone Layer and is relatively stable, which is why commercial jet aircraft often fly in its lower parts to avoid weather disturbances.

• The Mesosphere is the third layer where temperatures drop to their lowest in the atmosphere and where most meteors burn up upon entry.

• The Thermosphere is a high-altitude layer where temperatures increase significantly with height and contains the Ionosphere, which is crucial for radio communication.

• The boundary that separates the troposphere from the stratosphere is called the Tropopause.

• The height of the troposphere varies with latitude; it is thickest at the Equator (about 18 km) and thinnest at the Poles (about 8 km) due to the differences in solar heating and centrifugal force.

• The Exosphere is the outermost layer of the Earth's atmosphere, extending into space, where the air is extremely thin.

• The atmosphere is composed mainly of Nitrogen (78%) and Oxygen (21%), with trace amounts of other gases like Argon and Carbon Dioxide.

11. Which of the following is a characteristic feature of meristematic tissues in plants?

A. Large vacuoles and thick cell walls

B. Absence of nucleus and presence of lignified walls

C. Actively dividing cells with dense cytoplasm and prominent nuclei

D. Dead cells that provide mechanical support

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Actively dividing cells with dense cytoplasm and prominent nuclei. Key Points Additional Information

• Meristematic tissues consist of actively dividing cells that are responsible for plant growth.

• Cells in meristematic tissues have a dense cytoplasm and lack large vacuoles, which allows efficient division.

• These cells possess prominent nuclei to control cell division and metabolic activities.

• The cell walls in meristematic tissues are thin and non-lignified, facilitating flexibility during cell division.

• Meristematic tissues are located in specific regions such as apical meristems, lateral meristems, and intercalary meristems.

• Apical Meristems: Found at the tips of roots and shoots, responsible for primary growth (increase in length).

• Lateral Meristems: Located in the sides of stems and roots, responsible for secondary growth (increase in thickness).

• Intercalary Meristems: Present at the base of leaves or internodes, aiding in regrowth of grass and other monocots.

• Meristematic cells lack large vacuoles because vacuoles are primarily used for storage, which is not needed during active division.

• Meristematic cells eventually differentiate into permanent tissues, which perform specific functions such as support, transport, or photosynthesis.

12. Deoxygenated blood from the body enters through which of the following chamber of the heart of humans?

A. Right atrium

B. Right ventricle

C. Left atrium

D. Left ventricle

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is Right atrium. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Deoxygenated blood from various parts of the body returns to the heart through two large veins - the superior vena cava and the inferior vena cava.

• The superior vena cava collects blood from the upper part of the body (head, neck, arms), while the inferior vena cava collects blood from the lower part of the body (abdomen, legs).

• Both these veins empty deoxygenated blood into the right atrium, which is the first chamber of the heart to receive this blood.

• From the right atrium, the blood is pushed into the right ventricle during atrial contraction.

• The right atrium plays a crucial role in the circulation of blood as it acts as a receiving chamber for deoxygenated blood, initiating the pulmonary circulation process to re-oxygenate the blood.

• Heart Chambers and Their Functions: The human heart has four chambers: the right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, and left ventricle.

• The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it into the right ventricle.

• The right ventricle pumps the blood to the lungs for oxygenation through the pulmonary artery.

• The left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and sends it to the left ventricle.

• The left ventricle pumps the oxygenated blood to the entire body through the aorta.

• Pulmonary Circulation: Deoxygenated blood is pumped from the right ventricle to the lungs through the pulmonary artery.

• In the lungs, carbon dioxide is exchanged for oxygen in the alveoli (tiny air sacs).

• The oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium of the heart via the pulmonary veins.

• Systemic Circulation: Oxygenated blood is pumped from the left ventricle to the entire body through the aorta.

• The body tissues utilize oxygen and nutrients from the blood, and in return, the blood picks up carbon dioxide and waste products.

• Deoxygenated blood is then returned to the right atrium through the superior and inferior vena cavae.

• Valves of the Heart: The heart has four major valves to ensure the unidirectional flow of blood: tricuspid valve, pulmonary valve, mitral valve, and aortic valve.

• The tricuspid valve is located between the right atrium and right ventricle, preventing backflow of blood.

• The pulmonary valve lies between the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery, regulating blood flow to the lungs.

• The right atrium is part of the systemic circulation and acts as the entry point for blood returning from the body.

• The separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in the heart's chambers is critical for efficient circulation in humans and other mammals.

• The heart beats approximately 72 times per minute on average, ensuring continuous circulation of blood.

• Any obstruction in the flow of blood to or from the right atrium can lead to serious cardiovascular conditions, such as right heart failure.

18. Mechanical energy is mainly composed of how many types?

• Potential energy is the energy an object possesses due to its position or configuration. For instance, water stored in a dam at height or a stretched spring has potential energy.

• The sum of kinetic and potential energy in a system constitutes its total mechanical energy.

20. Two resistors, one with a resistance of 2 ohms and the other with a resistance of 4 ohms, are connected in parallel to a 6-volt battery. What is the total current flowing through the circuit?

A. 1 Ampere

B. 2 Amperes

C. 3 Amperes

D. 4.5 Amperes

Answer D

Solution

The correct answer is 4.5 Amperes. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The two resistors of 2 ohms and 4 ohms are connected in parallel. In a parallel circuit, the total resistance is calculated using the formula: 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2.

• Substituting the values: 1/Rtotal = 1/2 + 1/4 = 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4. Thus, Rtotal = 4/3 ohms.

• The circuit is powered by a 6-volt battery. Using Ohm’s Law (V = IR), the total current can be calculated: Itotal = V/Rtotal. Substitute the values: Itotal = 6 / (4/3) = 6 × 3/4 = 4.5 Amperes.

• Hence, the total current flowing through the circuit is 4.5 Amperes.

• Each resistor shares the current based on its resistance. The current through the 2-ohm resistor is: I = V/R = 6/2 = 3 Amperes. The current through the 4-ohm resistor is: I = V/R = 6/4 = 1.5 Amperes. Adding these gives 4.5 Amperes, verifying the calculation.

• Ohm’s Law: Ohm’s Law states that the current flowing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points, provided the temperature remains constant.

• The formula is: V = IR, where V is voltage, I is current, and R is resistance.

• Parallel Circuit Properties: In a parallel circuit, the voltage across all components remains the same.

• The total resistance in a parallel circuit is always less than the smallest individual resistance.

• The total current is the sum of the currents through each parallel branch.

• Resistor Applications: Resistors are widely used in electrical circuits for current regulation, voltage division, and protection against overcurrent.

• They are categorized as fixed resistors (constant resistance) or variable resistors (adjustable resistance).

• Battery Characteristics: A battery is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy. The voltage rating of a battery determines the electric potential difference it can provide.

• Common batteries include AA, AAA, lithium-ion, and lead-acid batteries, used in various applications like household electronics, vehicles, and industrial equipment.

• Series vs. Parallel Circuits: Series Circuit: The current remains constant throughout the circuit, and the total resistance is the sum of individual resistances (Rtotal = R1 + R2 + …).

• Parallel Circuit: The voltage remains constant across all branches, and the total resistance is calculated using 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ….

• Electrical Safety: To prevent electrical hazards, circuits are often equipped with fuses, circuit breakers, and insulation materials.

• Proper resistance calculations are critical to ensure safe operation and avoid overheating.

• Practical Examples: Parallel circuits are commonly found in home electrical systems, ensuring that individual appliances function independently.

• Series circuits are used in applications like string lights, where the current flows through all components sequentially.

24. What is the change of state directly from gas to solid without changing into liquid state known as?

A. Solidification

B. Deposition

C. Freezing

D. Evaporation

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Deposition. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Deposition is the process where a substance changes its state directly from a gas to a solid without passing through the liquid phase.

• This process is the reverse of sublimation, where a solid turns directly into a gas.

• Deposition occurs under specific conditions, such as when the temperature decreases or pressure changes, and the molecules lose enough energy to bond and form a solid.

• An example of deposition is the formation of frost on surfaces. Water vapor in the air directly changes to ice crystals on cold surfaces.

• This phenomenon is commonly observed in nature and is used in industrial processes such as thin-film deposition in semiconductor manufacturing.

• Solidification: Solidification is the process where a liquid changes its state to a solid, usually by cooling.

• It is different from deposition because it involves the intermediate liquid state.

• An example of solidification is the freezing of water to form ice.

• Freezing: Freezing is specifically the change of a liquid into a solid by lowering the temperature.

• It is a subset of solidification and does not involve skipping the liquid phase, unlike deposition.

• Evaporation: Evaporation is the process where a liquid turns into a gas at temperatures below its boiling point.

• This is the opposite of condensation, and it does not involve the solid state.

• Applications of Deposition: Used in chemical vapor deposition (CVD) to create thin films on surfaces in the manufacturing of electronics.

• Helps in the formation of frost and snowflakes in nature.

• Used in artificial diamond synthesis and coating processes.

• Frost formation: Frost forms through deposition when water vapor in the air directly changes into ice crystals due to cold temperatures.

• Temperature and pressure effects: Deposition is highly dependent on environmental conditions like temperature and pressure, as they influence the energy of molecules.

• Industrial usage: Deposition processes like sputtering and vapor deposition are essential for creating thin films, coatings, and advanced materials in industries.

• Reverse of sublimation: Deposition is the reverse process of sublimation, highlighting the symmetry in phase transitions.

25. What is the function of the lysosomes in animal cells?

A. Transport proteins within the cell

B. Break down waste materials and cellular debris

C. Produce ATP through cellular respiration

D. Synthesize lipids

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Break down waste materials and cellular debris. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles found in animal cells. They are often referred to as the "suicide bags" of the cell due to their ability to break down cellular components.

• The primary function of lysosomes is to break down waste materials and cellular debris. They contain hydrolytic enzymes that are capable of digesting proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids.

• Lysosomes play a critical role in autophagy, a process where damaged organelles and unnecessary cellular components are digested and recycled to maintain cellular health.

• They are also involved in the immune response by destroying pathogens and microorganisms that enter the cell through processes like phagocytosis.

• Lysosomes contribute to cellular homeostasis by removing potentially harmful substances and preventing the accumulation of toxic materials. Hence, the correct answer is to break down waste materials and cellular debris.

• Structure of Lysosomes: Lysosomes are surrounded by a single phospholipid bilayer membrane and contain a variety of acid hydrolases that function optimally at a low pH (around 4.5-5).

• The acidic environment is maintained by proton pumps located on the lysosomal membrane.

• Functions of Lysosomes: Lysosomes are involved in processes like autophagy, where cellular components are recycled to maintain cell health.

• They also play a role in endocytosis and phagocytosis, where they digest substances that enter the cell.

• Example: Lysosomes in macrophages help break down pathogens during an immune response.

• Differences in Plant and Animal Cells: Plant cells generally lack lysosomes, but similar functions are performed by vacuoles.

• Animal cells rely on lysosomes for efficient waste management and recycling.

• Other Functions of Lysosomes: Lysosomes play a role in apoptosis, or programmed cell death, ensuring the removal of damaged or diseased cells.

• They are involved in the storage and release of enzymes required for breaking down extracellular materials.

• Examples of Lysosomal Disorders: Tay-Sachs disease: A genetic disorder caused by lysosomal enzyme deficiency leading to accumulation of harmful substances in neurons.

• Pompe disease: Caused by a deficiency in the enzyme required for glycogen breakdown within lysosomes.
