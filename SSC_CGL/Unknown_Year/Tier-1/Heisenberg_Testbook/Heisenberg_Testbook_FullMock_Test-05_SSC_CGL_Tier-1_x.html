• Resistance of a wire is determined by the formula: R = ρ × (L/A), where: R: Resistance of the wire

• ρ: Resistivity (material property, constant for a given material)

• L: Length of the wire

• A: Cross-sectional area of the wire

• When the length of the wire is doubled, the new length becomes 2L.

• When the cross-sectional area is halved, the new area becomes A/2.

• Substituting these changes into the formula for resistance: New Resistance (R') = ρ × ((2L) / (A/2))

• R' = ρ × (2L × 2 / A)

• R' = 4 × (ρ × L/A)

• Thus, the resistance of the wire increases to four times its original value.

• Factors affecting resistance: Length of the wire (L): Resistance is directly proportional to the length. Doubling the length doubles the resistance.

• Cross-sectional area (A): Resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area. Halving the area doubles the resistance.

• Material of the wire: Different materials have different resistivities (ρ), affecting their resistance.

• Temperature: Resistance changes with temperature, usually increasing for conductors as temperature rises.

• Ohm's Law: Ohm's law states that the current (I) through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage (V) across the two points.

• Mathematically: V = IR, where R is the resistance.

• Applications of resistance: Resistance is utilized in devices like resistors, heaters, and filaments in light bulbs to control current flow.

• Higher resistance is used in devices to convert electrical energy into heat (e.g., electric irons).

• Resistivity: It is a material-specific property that quantifies how strongly a given material opposes the flow of electric current.

• Unit: Ohm meter (Ω·m).

• Materials with low resistivity, like copper and silver, are good conductors, while materials with high resistivity, like rubber, are insulators.
