1. The national highways in India constitute only about _________ of the total road length but carry almost 40% of the road traffic.

A. 2%

B. 8%

C. 12%

D. 18%

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is 1) 2%. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• National Highways in India comprise around 2% of the total road network in the country. Despite the small percentage, they play a vital role in transportation and connectivity.

• Although they constitute only 2% of the total road length, they bear almost 40% of the total road traffic, underlining their importance in national and inter-state connectivity.

• The National Highway Authority of India (NHAI) is responsible for the development, maintenance, and management of these highways under the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways.

• National highways connect major cities, towns, ports, and industrial hubs, making them the backbone of the Indian economy by facilitating trade and transportation.

• India has around 1,44,634 kilometers of national highways (as of 2023), which includes expressways and high-capacity roads designed for long-distance travel.

• Functions and Importance of National Highways: National highways are crucial for economic growth as they facilitate the movement of goods and services across the country.

• They provide connectivity between state capitals, major ports, and international borders, ensuring seamless inter-state and international trade.

• They also play a vital role in reducing travel time and fuel consumption, thereby contributing to environmental sustainability.

• Development Programs: The Bharatmala Pariyojana, launched in 2017, is a centrally sponsored scheme aimed at improving the efficiency of road traffic movement by bridging critical infrastructure gaps.

• The program focuses on the construction of economic corridors, inter-corridors, and feeder routes to ensure better connectivity and reduce travel time.

• Other Road Categories in India: State Highways: These connect major towns and district headquarters within states and contribute about 3% of the road network in India.

• District Roads: These roads connect district headquarters to rural areas and account for around 10% of the total road length.

• Rural Roads: These roads connect villages and are part of the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana for rural connectivity. They form a substantial portion of India’s road network.

• Global Comparison: India’s road network is the second-largest in the world, with the United States having the largest road network.

• However, India’s national highways contribute a smaller percentage compared to its total road network, highlighting the need for further expansion and development.

• Expressways: These are a subset of national highways designed for high-speed travel, such as the Delhi-Mumbai Expressway, which is under development.

• Challenges: National highways face challenges such as overcrowding, maintenance issues, and land acquisition problems for expansion and development.

• Government Initiatives: Programs like the National Highways Development Project (NHDP) aim to upgrade and expand the national highway network to meet future demands.

• Economic Impact: Upgraded highways have a direct impact on GDP growth, employment generation, and regional development by enhancing connectivity and infrastructure.

3. In which type of geological formation are gorges most commonly developed?

A. Soft sediment

B. Hard rocks

C. Glacial till

D. Volcanic ash

Answer B

Solution

Gorges are most commonly developed in hard rock formations due to their resistance to erosion, allowing rivers to carve deep, narrow valleys with steep, vertical walls. Hard rocks like granite and basalt maintain the gorge's structure over time, unlike soft sediment, glacial till, or volcanic ash, which erode easily.

4. In basketball, how many players usually make up each team on the court during official play?

A. 7

B. 6

C. 8

D. 5

Answer D

Solution

The correct answer is 5 players. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• In official basketball games, each team is allowed to have 5 players on the court at any given time. This is the standard rule as per the regulations of international basketball governing bodies like FIBA (International Basketball Federation) and the NBA (National Basketball Association).

• The 5 players on the court typically include specific roles: point guard, shooting guard, small forward, power forward, and center. Each position has unique responsibilities and contributes to the team’s strategy.

• If a team has fewer than 5 players available during the game (due to fouls, injuries, etc.), it can still continue playing. However, a team must have at least 2 players on the court to avoid forfeiting the match.

• Basketball is designed to be played with 5 players per team to maintain a balance between offense and defense, ensuring a fast-paced and competitive game.

• The rule of 5 players has been in place since the sport's inception in 1891, when Dr. James Naismith invented basketball in the United States.

• Basketball Positions: Point Guard: Often considered the "floor general," this player handles the ball, organizes plays, and ensures the team’s offense runs smoothly.

• Shooting Guard: Known for their scoring ability, especially from long-range. They complement the point guard in the backcourt.

• Small Forward: A versatile player who can score, defend, and assist. They often play both inside and outside roles.

• Power Forward: Typically stronger and taller, this player focuses on rebounding and scoring near the basket.

• Center: The tallest player on the team, they are responsible for blocking shots, rebounding, and scoring close to the hoop.

• Basketball Court Dimensions: According to FIBA standards, the court measures 28 meters in length and 15 meters in width. The NBA court is slightly larger at 94 feet by 50 feet.

• Substitution Rules: Teams can substitute players from the bench as needed. There is no limit to the number of substitutions during a game, but they must be made during stoppages in play.

• Game Duration: In FIBA games, the game consists of 4 quarters of 10 minutes each. The NBA, however, has 12-minute quarters, making the total game time longer.

• Team Composition: A basketball team typically consists of 12 active players (5 on the court and 7 substitutes). In professional leagues, teams may carry additional players on their roster.

• Historical Context: Basketball was invented in 1891 by Dr. James Naismith in Springfield, Massachusetts, USA. The original game featured 9 players per side, but this was later reduced to 5 for better gameplay.

• Fouls and Violations: A player fouls out of the game after committing 5 personal fouls in FIBA games or 6 personal fouls in the NBA.

• Professional Leagues: The NBA, established in 1946, is the most prominent basketball league globally, while FIBA governs international competitions like the Olympics and the FIBA Basketball World Cup.

• Popularity: Basketball is one of the most popular sports globally, with millions of players and fans across countries like the United States, China, the Philippines, Spain, and Australia.

• Trivia: The first basketball hoop was a peach basket, and the first game was played using a soccer ball before the basketball was specifically designed.

5. The Mughal architecture is best known for which of the following features?

A. Wooden beams

B. High domes and minarets

C. Flat roofs

D. Simple stone walls

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is High domes and minarets. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Mughal architecture is a remarkably symmetrical and decorative style of building that flourished in India under the Mughal Empire from the 16th to the 18th century.

• One of the most defining characteristics of this style is the use of high, bulbous domes (often double domes) and slender minarets at the corners of the main structures. These features provided a sense of grandeur and verticality to the buildings.

• The architecture is a unique synthesis of Islamic, Persian, Turkic, and Indian (Hindu) architectural elements. Notable examples include the Taj Mahal in Agra, completed around 1653, which features a massive central dome and four towering minarets.

• Architects primarily used red sandstone during the early phase (under Akbar) and transitioned to white marble during the peak of the empire (under Shah Jahan) to create intricate designs.

• Other prominent features include large vaulted gateways (such as the Buland Darwaza), massive halls, and delicate ornamentation like Pietra Dura (stone inlay work) and Jali (lattice screens).

• Evolution of the Style: The style began with Babur, but it was Humayun’s Tomb in Delhi (built in 1570) that introduced the Persian-style double dome and the Charbagh (four-part garden) layout to India.

• Under Akbar (r. 1556–1605), the use of red sandstone was dominant. He built the city of Fatehpur Sikri near Agra, showcasing a fusion of regional Indian styles with Islamic forms.

• The "Golden Age" of Mughal architecture occurred under Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658), who preferred white marble inlaid with semi-precious stones.

• Charbagh Layout: Mughal structures are often placed at the center or end of a Charbagh, a quadrilateral garden layout based on the four gardens of Paradise mentioned in the Quran. The gardens are divided by walkways or flowing water.

• Decline of the Style: Under Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707), the architectural activities slowed down. The Bibi Ka Maqbara in Aurangabad (built in 1678) is a notable structure from this era, though it is often seen as a less grand imitation of the Taj Mahal.

• Analysis of Options: Wooden beams: While wood was used in ancient Indian architecture and some regional styles, Mughal grandeur relied on arches, vaults, and stone lintels rather than exposed wooden beams as a defining feature. Hence, this option is incorrect.

• High domes and minarets: These are the iconic hallmarks of Mughal tombs and mosques, providing the scale and silhouette associated with the empire. Hence, this statement is correct.

• Flat roofs: While some domestic structures had flat roofs, the representative architecture of the Mughals (mosques and tombs) is famous for its curved domes and vaulted ceilings. Hence, this statement is incorrect.

• Simple stone walls: Mughal walls were rarely "simple"; they were almost always decorated with carvings, calligraphy, tiles, or Pietra Dura. Hence, this statement is incorrect.

• Pietra Dura: This technique involved embedding highly polished colored stones like lapis lazuli, jade, and crystal into white marble to create floral and geometric patterns, most famously seen in the Taj Mahal.

6. What is the primary sector of the Indian economy mainly concerned with?

A. Manufacturing

B. Mining

C. Agriculture

D. Banking

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Agriculture. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The Primary Sector of the economy is responsible for the extraction and harvesting of natural resources directly from the earth. In the context of India, this sector is mainly concerned with Agriculture, which includes crop production, animal husbandry, forestry, and fishing. Hence, the option Agriculture is correct.

• Agriculture serves as the backbone of the Indian economy, employing approximately 44% to 48% of the total workforce as of recent labor surveys, making it the largest employer in the country.

• The primary sector provides the essential raw materials required by the secondary sector for manufacturing and processing activities. For example, cotton from agriculture is used in textile industries.

• Historically, the share of the primary sector in India's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) was very high during Independence (1947), but it has gradually shifted to around 18% to 20% in the financial year 2022-23.

• Manufacturing involves the conversion of raw materials into finished goods using machinery and labor, which falls under the Secondary Sector. Hence, the option Manufacturing is incorrect.

• Mining involves the extraction of minerals and ores. While it is technically a primary activity, in the Indian economic classification, the term "Primary Sector" is most significantly synonymous with Agriculture and allied activities due to its massive scale. Hence, the option Mining is less accurate compared to Agriculture in this context.

• Banking is a service-based activity that facilitates financial transactions and credit, which is a core component of the Tertiary Sector. Hence, the option Banking is incorrect.

• Secondary Sector: This sector adds value to natural resources by transforming them into products through industrial activity. It includes manufacturing units, construction, and electricity, gas, and water supply. It contributes roughly 25% to 28% to the Indian GDP.

• Tertiary Sector: Also known as the Service Sector, it provides support to both the primary and secondary sectors. Key components include Banking, Transportation, Education, Healthcare, and Information Technology (IT). It is currently the largest contributor to India's GDP, accounting for over 53%.

• Quaternary and Quinary Sectors: These are specialized branches of the tertiary sector. The Quaternary sector focuses on knowledge-based services like R&D and software development, while the Quinary sector involves high-level decision-making by government officials and top corporate executives.

• Economic Transition: Unlike many developed nations that moved from primary to secondary and then to tertiary, India experienced a "leapfrogging" effect where the Services sector grew rapidly while the Manufacturing sector stayed relatively stagnant.

• Green Revolution: Initiated in the 1960s, this movement significantly boosted agricultural productivity in India through the use of High Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds, modern irrigation, and fertilizers.

• Disguised Unemployment: A major feature of the Indian primary sector where more people are working in Agriculture than are actually required, leading to zero marginal productivity for extra workers.

• Government Initiatives: To support the primary sector, the Government of India has launched schemes like PM-KISAN (direct income support), Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (crop insurance), and e-NAM (electronic national agriculture market).

• GDP vs Employment: There is a significant structural disparity in India where the primary sector employs nearly half the population but contributes less than one-fifth of the national income, indicating low per-capita productivity compared to the Services sector.

7. Why did Mahatma Gandhi call for a campaign against the Rowlatt Act?

A. During the non-cooperation movement, thousands of Indians were kept in jail

B. British censorship of press and permitted detention without trial

C. Hundreds of people were killed in the Jallianwala Bagh massacre

D. Sir Sidney Rowlatt was a Britisher

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is British censorship of press and permitted detention without trial. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The Rowlatt Act, officially known as the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act of 1919, was a legislative act passed by the Imperial Legislative Council in Delhi on 18th March 1919.

• Mahatma Gandhi called for a nationwide campaign against this act because it authorized the British Government to imprison people suspected of terrorism for up to two years without any trial. This violated the basic principle of civil liberty and judicial fairness. Hence, the statement regarding detention without trial is correct.

• The act also provided for the censorship of the press, allowing the colonial administration to control and suppress the flow of information and nationalist propaganda. Hence, the statement regarding censorship of the press is correct.

• The Non-cooperation movement (1920-22) happened after the Rowlatt Satyagraha; therefore, the jailing of Indians during that movement was not the reason for Gandhi's initial call against the Rowlatt Act. Hence, statement 1 is incorrect.

• While the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13th April 1919) was a direct consequence of the protests against the Rowlatt Act and the arrest of leaders like Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal, it was not the reason why Gandhi initially called for the campaign. Hence, statement 3 is incorrect.

• Although Sir Sidney Rowlatt was indeed a British judge who headed the committee, his nationality was not the primary reason for the protest; rather, it was the draconian nature of the laws he recommended. Hence, statement 4 is incorrect.

• The Rowlatt Committee: The act was based on the report of the Sedition Committee of 1918, chaired by Sir Sidney Rowlatt. The committee was tasked with investigating the revolutionary conspiracies of the Indian nationalist movement and suggesting measures to curb them.

• The Black Act: Gandhi and other Indian leaders referred to the Rowlatt Act as the Black Act. The popular slogan associated with the protest was No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal, meaning there was no right to argument, no right to a lawyer, and no right to appeal against the detention.

• Rowlatt Satyagraha: Gandhi organized the Satyagraha Sabha in Bombay to direct the movement. He called for a nationwide hartal (strike) on 6th April 1919, which was observed as a day of humiliation and prayer and a protest against the unjust law.

• Resignations: In protest against the passing of the act, prominent Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council, including Madan Mohan Malaviya, Mazhar-ul-Haq, and Mohammed Ali Jinnah, resigned from their seats.

• Impact on National Movement: The Rowlatt Satyagraha was the first all-India struggle against the British government, although it was largely restricted to cities. It marked the transition of the Indian national movement from an elite-driven struggle to a mass-based movement under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi.

• Punjab Unrest: The protests were particularly intense in Punjab, especially in Amritsar and Lahore. The government responded with martial law, which eventually culminated in the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre on 13th April 1919, where General Dyer ordered troops to fire on a peaceful gathering.

• Withdrawal of Satyagraha: Seeing the violence that erupted following the massacre and the government's brutal repression, Mahatma Gandhi withdrew the Rowlatt Satyagraha on 18th April 1919, calling it a Himalayan Blunder because the people were not yet fully trained in the methods of non-violence.

• Legislative Context: The act was passed despite the unanimous opposition of every single Indian member of the council, highlighting the autocratic nature of British rule in India during that period.

9. An Indian state can be divided or its boundary altered by which of the following?

A. The State Legislature

B. Parliament

C. President

D. Supreme Court

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Parliament. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Article 3 of the Constitution of India grants the Parliament of India the exclusive authority to create new states, alter state boundaries, or change the name of an existing state.

• The process involves introducing a bill in Parliament for the reorganization or alteration of states. The President's recommendation is mandatory before introducing such a bill. The state legislature's opinion is sought but does not carry binding authority.

• Parliament can reorganize states by separating territories, merging existing states, or annexing territories to a particular state. This ensures flexibility in addressing administrative needs or sociopolitical changes.

• Historical examples: The formation of states like Jharkhand (2000), Telangana (2014), and Chhattisgarh (2000) showcases Parliament's pivotal role in state reorganization.

• The Supreme Court does not play a direct role in altering state boundaries. Similarly, the State Legislature lacks authority to independently reorganize or create new states. Hence, the correct answer is Parliament.

• Article 3 of the Constitution: Article 3 provides a framework for the reorganization of states. It allows Parliament to create new states, alter boundaries, and rename states based on administrative, geographical, or socio-political needs.

• Before introducing a bill for state reorganization, it is necessary to obtain the President's recommendation. The opinion of the concerned state's legislature is sought, but Parliament has the final authority.

• Historical Examples of State Reorganization: 1956: The States Reorganization Act was passed, creating states based on linguistic lines such as Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Andhra Pradesh.

• 2000: Three new states — Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and Uttarakhand — were formed to address specific sociopolitical demands.

• 2014: Telangana was carved out of Andhra Pradesh to meet long-standing demands for statehood.

• Role of the President: The President of India must recommend a bill for state reorganization before its introduction in Parliament.

• The President refers the bill to the concerned state legislature for its opinion, but Parliament holds the final decision-making authority.

• State Legislature and Supreme Court: The State Legislature can provide its opinion on reorganization but lacks authority to independently alter state boundaries or create new states.

• The Supreme Court does not have direct jurisdiction to reorganize states but can review disputes arising from state reorganization acts.

• Constitutional Provisions: Article 2: Parliament can admit new territories into the Union of India.

• Article 4: Any law under Articles 2 and 3 is not considered an amendment to the Constitution under Article 368.

• Challenges in State Reorganization: State reorganization often leads to disputes over resources, geographical boundaries, and administrative control.

• Example: The bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana in 2014 led to disputes over water-sharing agreements and capital city allocation.

• Modern Implications: State reorganization is crucial for addressing administrative inefficiencies and ensuring balanced regional development.

• It is also significant for responding to cultural, linguistic, and political demands, ensuring harmony within the Union.

11. What is the primary focus of BIMSTEC?

A. Military cooperation.

B. Economic and technical cooperation

C. Space exploration

D. Cultural exchange

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Economic and technical cooperation. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• BIMSTEC, or the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation, is a regional organization comprising seven member countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and Thailand.

• The primary focus of BIMSTEC is on fostering economic and technical cooperation among member nations, with the aim of promoting shared growth and development.

• The organization aims to act as a bridge between South and Southeast Asia, leveraging the Bay of Bengal region as an area of cooperation and connectivity.

• BIMSTEC identifies key areas of cooperation, including trade, investment, technology, energy, transport, and tourism. These sectors are critical for improving economic ties and technical collaboration among member countries.

• The organization also focuses on enhancing regional integration and addressing common challenges such as poverty, climate change, and natural disasters.

• BIMSTEC is not a military or security alliance; its focus remains primarily on economic and technical areas, distinguishing it from other regional organizations.

• The organization facilitates cooperation in science and technology, including the exchange of expertise and resources to address technological needs in member states.

• Formation of BIMSTEC: BIMSTEC was established in June 1997 through the Bangkok Declaration. Initially, it was known as the BIST-EC (Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, and Thailand Economic Cooperation). Later, Myanmar joined in 1997, followed by Nepal and Bhutan in 2004, and the name was changed to BIMSTEC.

• The organization aims to promote sub-regional cooperation among countries bordering the Bay of Bengal, focusing on economic and technical collaboration.

• Key Areas of Cooperation: Trade and Investment: BIMSTEC works to enhance trade facilitation, reduce trade barriers, and promote investment opportunities among member countries.

• Connectivity: Improving transport and infrastructure networks, such as highways and maritime routes, is a crucial aspect of BIMSTEC's agenda.

• Energy Cooperation: The organization focuses on enhancing energy security, including the development of renewable energy sources like solar and wind power.

• Difference from Other Organizations: Unlike organizations like SAARC, which focus on political cooperation, BIMSTEC is primarily focused on economic and technical collaboration.

• BIMSTEC does not have a military focus, distinguishing it from organizations such as NATO or ASEAN’s security framework.

• Key Facts about BIMSTEC: BIMSTEC covers a population of approximately 1.7 billion people (around 22% of the world's population) and has a combined GDP of over US $4 trillion.

• The headquarters of BIMSTEC is located in Dhaka, Bangladesh.

• BIMSTEC promotes people-to-people contact through cultural exchanges, tourism, and educational initiatives.

• Challenges and Opportunities: Challenges include regional disparities, political differences, and the need for stronger institutional frameworks.

• However, BIMSTEC offers significant opportunities for regional integration, sustainable development, and addressing shared challenges like climate change and disaster management.

13. Bihu songs are a part of which Indian state's folk tradition?

A. Assam

B. Punjab

C. Gujarat

D. Odisha

Answer A

Solution

The correct answer is Assam. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Bihu songs, known as Bihu Geet, are a core component of the folk tradition of Assam, a state in Northeast India. These songs are performed during the Bihu festival, which is the most important cultural celebration for the Assamese community.

• The songs are traditionally characterized by themes of love, eroticism, nature, and the agricultural cycle. They reflect the joys and sorrows of the rural life of Assam.

• Bihu songs are accompanied by the Bihu dance, which involves energetic steps and rapid hand movements. This folk tradition is inclusive, performed by both young men and women in the open fields or Bakori.

• The musical accompaniment for these songs includes traditional instruments like the Dhol (a percussion instrument), Pepa (a hornpipe made from buffalo horn), Gogona (a reed/bamboo instrument), Toka (a bamboo clapper), and Bahi (flute).

• The tradition is deeply rooted in the fertility cult, where the music and dance are believed to stimulate the earth's fertility and ensure a bountiful harvest. Hence, the correct answer is Assam.

• Punjab: This northern state is famous for folk traditions like Bhangra and Gidda. Bhangra is typically performed by men during the Baisakhi festival to celebrate the harvest, while Gidda is a graceful dance performed by women.

• Gujarat: Known for its vibrant folk dances such as Garba and Dandiya Raas, which are performed extensively during the Navratri festival. These traditions involve circular movements and the use of sticks (Dandiya) to honor Goddess Durga.

• Odisha: This state is home to the classical dance Odissi and various folk traditions like Sambalpuri, Chhau, and Ghumura. Sambalpuri songs and dances are particularly famous for their rhythmic beats and the use of the Mahuri and Dhol.

• Assam's Three Bihus: The state celebrates three types of Bihu in a year. Bohag Bihu (or Rongali Bihu) in April marks the Assamese New Year; Kati Bihu (or Kongali Bihu) in October is a more solemn observation related to crop protection; and Magh Bihu (or Bhogali Bihu) in January is a harvest festival centered around feasting.

• In April 2023, Assam created a Guinness World Record when over 11,000 dancers and drummers performed the Bihu dance and played the Dhol at the Sarusajai Stadium in Guwahati.

• The Pepa, an essential instrument in Bihu songs, is traditionally crafted from the horn of a dead buffalo, and its piercing sound is synonymous with the spring season in Assam.

15. The Deccan Traps were formed as a result of which geological event?

B. Volcanic eruptions

C. Glacial movement

D. Tectonic plate collision

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Volcanic eruptions. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The Deccan Traps are a massive Large Igneous Province located in west-central India. They were formed by the accumulation of multiple layers of solidified flood basalt resulting from intense volcanic activity.

• The primary geological event responsible for their formation was a series of fissure-type volcanic eruptions. These occurred as the Indian Plate moved over the Reunion hotspot in the Indian Ocean during its northward drift.

• Earthquakes are seismic vibrations caused by the sudden release of energy in the Earth's lithosphere. While they occur in volcanic regions, they do not deposit the massive layers of igneous rock that define the Deccan plateau. Hence, this option is incorrect.

• Volcanic eruptions involved the outpouring of low-viscosity basaltic lava that spread over vast areas, cooling to form the characteristic step-like topography of the region. Hence, this option is correct.

• Glacial movement refers to the flow of ice masses which shapes the landscape through erosion and the deposition of sediments like moraines. The Deccan region shows no evidence of massive glaciation during the Mesozoic Era. Hence, this option is incorrect.

• The Deccan Traps are one of the few examples of a continental flood basalt province on Earth, comparable in scale to the Siberian Traps in Russia.

• The volcanic activity is estimated to have lasted for a duration ranging from 30,000 to 1,000,000 years, occurring in several distinct pulses of high-volume eruptions.

23. Who introduced the Ryotwari System in India?

A. Lord Cornwallis

B. Thomas Munro

D. Robert Clive

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Thomas Munro. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The Ryotwari System was a land revenue system in British India, introduced to establish a direct relationship between the government and the cultivators. It was primarily introduced by Sir Thomas Munro and Captain Alexander Read at the end of the 18th century.

• Thomas Munro, who served as the Governor of Madras from 1820 to 1827, is credited with the formal implementation of this system across the Madras Presidency in 1820. Hence, the correct answer is Thomas Munro.

• The system was first trialed by Captain Alexander Read in the Baramahal region (modern-day Tamil Nadu) in 1792 after the Third Anglo-Mysore War.

• Under the Ryotwari settlement, the Ryots (peasants) were recognized as the individual owners of the land. They had the right to sell, mortgage, or transfer the land as long as they paid the fixed revenue to the East India Company.

• Unlike the Zamindari System, there were no middlemen or intermediaries involved. The revenue was collected directly by British officials, and the rates were revised periodically, usually every 20 to 30 years, based on soil fertility and crop type.

• Lord Cornwallis: He is famous for introducing the Permanent Settlement (also known as the Zamindari System) in Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha in 1793. In this system, Zamindars were made hereditary owners of the land, and the revenue amount was fixed permanently.

• Warren Hastings: He was the first Governor-General of Bengal (1773–1785). He introduced the Ijaradari System in 1772, which involved auctioning the right to collect land revenue to the highest bidder for a period of five years.

• Robert Clive: He served as the Governor of Bengal twice and established the Dual Government system in 1765 after the Battle of Buxar. Under this, the Nawab held the Nizamat (administrative) powers, while the British held the Diwani (revenue collection) rights.

• Mahalwari System: This was another major land revenue system introduced by Holt Mackenzie in 1822. It was implemented in the North-Western Provinces, Punjab, and parts of Central India, where the unit of revenue assessment was the village or Mahal.

• Geographical Coverage: The Ryotwari System covered approximately 51% of the territory under British rule, including the Madras Presidency, Bombay Presidency, Assam, and Coorg.

• Revenue Rates: The revenue demand was exceptionally high, often reaching 50% for dry lands and 60% for irrigated lands. This high demand often led to the exploitation of peasants by moneylenders when they could not meet the British demands.

• Economic Impact: While the system aimed to protect the Ryots from Zamindars, the rigid collection methods and high taxes resulted in large-scale rural indebtedness and land alienation, as British officials were often more oppressive than the former intermediaries.

• Legal Status: The Ryot was considered the proprietor of the land, but this ownership was conditional. Failure to pay the tax resulted in immediate eviction by the Government.
