2. In Draviḍa temple architecture, what is the central tower above the sanctum called?

A. Gopuram

B. Shikhara

C. Vimāna

D. Mandapa

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Vimāna. Key Points Additional Information

• In Draviḍa temple architecture, the Vimāna refers to the central tower or spire located directly above the sanctum (garbhagriha).

• The term "Vimāna" is derived from Sanskrit, meaning "a structure or a vehicle," symbolizing the heavenly chariot of the deity.

• The Vimāna is pyramidal in shape and consists of multiple tiers, often highly ornate with sculptural details representing mythological themes and deities.

• It is distinct from the Gopuram (gateway tower), which is located at the temple's entrance, and serves as the primary architectural feature emphasizing the sanctity of the shrine.

• Famous examples of Vimānas include the ones at the Brihadeeswarar Temple in Thanjavur and the Gangaikonda Cholapuram Temple, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

• Gopuram: The Gopuram is a monumental tower located at the entrance of a Dravidian-style temple.

• It serves as a gateway and is often adorned with intricate carvings, sculptures, and vibrant colors, depicting scenes from Hindu mythology.

• The Gopuram is typically taller and more visually striking than the Vimāna, symbolizing the grandeur of the temple complex.

• Examples include the Meenakshi Amman Temple's Gopuram in Madurai and the Sri Ranganathaswamy Temple's Gopuram in Srirangam.

• Shikhara: Shikhara is a term used in Nāgara (North Indian) temple architecture for the spire or tower above the sanctum.

• Unlike the pyramidal Vimāna in Draviḍa style, the Shikhara in Nāgara style is curvilinear and tapering.

• It symbolizes Mount Meru, the sacred cosmic mountain in Hindu cosmology.

• An example of Shikhara can be seen in the Kandariya Mahadeva Temple in Khajuraho.

• Mandapa: The Mandapa is a pillared hall or pavilion in a temple, used for rituals, ceremonies, and gatherings.

• It is located in front of the sanctum (garbhagriha) and serves as a space for devotees to assemble and perform prayers.

• Mandapas can be ornate and are often constructed in different forms, such as open or closed halls.

• Examples include the 100-pillared Mandapa in the Meenakshi Amman Temple.

• Overview of Draviḍa Temple Architecture: Draviḍa architecture is one of the three main temple architectural styles in India, predominantly found in Tamil Nadu.

• It is characterized by pyramidal Vimānas, elaborate Gopurams, and intricately carved pillars.

• Key components include the sanctum (garbhagriha), Mandapas (halls), and large temple tanks for ritualistic purposes.

• Famous examples are the temples of the Chola, Pandya, and Vijayanagara dynasties.

4. Which Mahājanapada emerged as the most dominant political power between the 6th–4th century BCE?

A. Gandhāra

B. Avanti

C. Magadha

D. Kuru

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Magadha. Key Points Additional Information

• Magadha emerged as the most dominant political power between the 6th–4th century BCE due to its strategic location and fertile land, which supported agriculture and trade.

• The rulers of Magadha, such as Bimbisara and Ajatashatru, adopted policies of expansionism and maintained strong armies equipped with advanced weaponry like war chariots and elephants.

• Magadha’s capital cities, first at Rajagriha and later at Pataliputra, became administrative and cultural centers, contributing to its dominance.

• The kingdom played a pivotal role in the spread of Jainism and Buddhism, with many significant events in the lives of Mahavira and Gautama Buddha occurring in Magadha.

• Magadha later formed the base for the establishment of the Mauryan Empire, one of the largest and most powerful empires in ancient India.

• Gandhāra: Gandhāra was located in present-day Afghanistan and northern Pakistan, with its prominent cities being Taxila and Peshawar.

• It was known for its contribution to art and culture, particularly the Gandhāra School of Art, which blended Indian and Greco-Roman styles.

• While it was an important center of learning and trade, it did not achieve the level of political dominance seen in Magadha.

• Avanti: Avanti was one of the major Mahājanapadas, located in modern-day Madhya Pradesh, with its capital at Ujjain.

• It was a significant center of trade and commerce due to its location on important trade routes.

• Although Avanti was a powerful Mahājanapada, it eventually came under Magadha’s control during the rise of the Magadhan Empire.

• Kuru: Kuru was a significant Mahājanapada situated in modern-day Haryana and Delhi regions.

• It is historically important as the setting for the epic Mahabharata and was known for its contributions to Vedic culture.

• However, by the 6th century BCE, its political significance had declined compared to other Mahājanapadas like Magadha and Avanti.

• In India, property transfer is governed by statutes like the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, which is uniform and applicable to all citizens regardless of religion.

20. Opportunity cost refers to:

A. Production expenses

B. Next best alternative forgone

C. Labour cost

D. Total spending

Answer B

Solution

The correct answer is Next best alternative forgone. Key Points Additional Information

• Opportunity cost refers to the value of the next best alternative that is forgone when a choice is made between multiple options.

• It emphasizes the concept of trade-offs in economics, where the decision to allocate resources to one option inherently means sacrificing the benefits of another option.

• This concept is widely used in decision-making processes, both for individuals and organizations, to assess the benefits and costs associated with various choices.

• Opportunity cost is not recorded in financial statements but is a crucial concept for evaluating efficiency and resource utilization.

• It helps in understanding the impact of limited resources, such as time, money, or effort, and guides optimal decision-making.

• Production Expenses: Production expenses refer to the direct and indirect costs incurred in manufacturing goods or providing services.

• These include raw material costs, labor costs, overhead expenses, and other related expenditures.

• Unlike opportunity cost, production expenses are recorded in financial statements and are directly linked to operational activities.

• Labour Cost: Labour cost refers to the total expenditure incurred by an organization to employ workers, including wages, salaries, and benefits.

• It is a critical component of production costs and is categorized into direct and indirect labor costs based on the nature of the work.

• Labour cost does not encompass the concept of trade-offs or forgone opportunities.

• Total Spending: Total spending refers to the sum of all expenditures made by an individual, organization, or economy over a specific period.

• This term is commonly used in economic analysis to measure consumption, investment, and government spending.

• Total spending is different from opportunity cost, as it focuses on monetary outflows rather than the value of forgone alternatives.

Solution

• Import substitution policy is an economic strategy aimed at reducing dependency on foreign imports by encouraging domestic production of goods.

• The primary objective of this policy was to protect infant industries, which are newly established industries that require government support to grow and compete in the market.

• This policy helps to create a self-reliant economy by fostering the development of local industries and reducing vulnerabilities to external economic shocks.

• It involves imposing tariffs, quotas, and restrictions on imports while providing subsidies and incentives to domestic industries.

• Countries like India adopted this policy extensively after independence as part of their economic planning to promote industrialization and reduce dependency on foreign goods.

• Policies promoting exports focus on enhancing production efficiency, improving product quality, and accessing international markets.

• Export promotion and import substitution are often seen as contrasting strategies, as the former focuses on global competitiveness while the latter prioritizes domestic production.

• Invite foreign capital This involves attracting foreign direct investment (FDI) or foreign portfolio investments (FPI) to boost domestic industrial growth and infrastructure development.

• While inviting foreign capital may complement industrialization, it is not a core focus of import substitution policies, which aim to reduce foreign dependency.

• Countries often pursue foreign capital through liberalization policies rather than protectionist strategies like import substitution.

• Increase imports Increasing imports is contrary to the goals of import substitution policy, which seeks to reduce reliance on imported goods.

• Encouraging imports is generally associated with trade liberalization policies to foster global trade and access to diverse goods and services.
