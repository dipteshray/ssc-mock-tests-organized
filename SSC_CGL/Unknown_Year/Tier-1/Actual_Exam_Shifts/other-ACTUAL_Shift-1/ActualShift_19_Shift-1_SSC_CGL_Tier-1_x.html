1. Which animal is absent from the carvings found on Mauryan stone pillars?

A. Lion

B. Bull

C. Elephant

D. Eagle

Answer D

Solution

The correct answer is Eagle. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• The Mauryan stone pillars are renowned for their exquisite carvings of animals, which symbolize different aspects of Indian culture and philosophy.

• Common animal motifs found on these pillars include the lion, bull, and elephant. These animals symbolized power, strength, and spirituality, respectively.

• The lion, for instance, is prominently featured on the Ashoka Pillar at Sarnath and represents courage, sovereignty, and the teachings of Buddhism.

• The bull symbolizes fertility, strength, and steadfastness, often associated with Lord Shiva in Indian culture.

• The elephant is a representation of wisdom and is often linked to Lord Buddha and his enlightenment journey.

• However, the eagle is not found on the Mauryan stone pillars. It is not a part of the animal motifs or cultural symbolisms used in Mauryan art.

• These pillars were erected by Emperor Ashoka during the 3rd century BCE and served as a medium to propagate his policies and the principles of Dhamma.

• Mauryan Stone Pillars: These pillars are one of the most significant examples of Mauryan art and architecture.

• They are monolithic, made from a single piece of stone, and often topped with intricately carved animal capitals.

• The inscriptions on these pillars are in Brahmi script and contain edicts of Emperor Ashoka.

• The pillars were primarily made of Chunar sandstone, known for its fine grain and durability.

• Symbolism of Animals on Pillars: The lion symbolizes courage and leadership. It is also associated with the Four Noble Truths of Buddhism.

• The bull represents agriculture and economic stability, central to the Mauryan empire's prosperity.

• The elephant is linked to mental strength and the spiritual journey of Lord Buddha.

• Significance of Ashoka Pillars: Ashoka pillars were erected as a means of spreading the message of Dhamma (righteousness).

• The most famous pillar, the Sarnath Lion Capital, is now the national emblem of India.

• These pillars reflect the influence of Persian and Hellenistic art, evident in their polished surfaces and animal capitals.

• Absence of the Eagle: The eagle, though significant in some other cultures and mythologies, is not a part of Mauryan symbolism or art.

• It was not used as a cultural or religious motif during Emperor Ashoka's reign.

• Ashoka’s Edicts: These inscriptions reflect Ashoka's commitment to non-violence, tolerance, and the welfare of all living beings.

• Spread of Buddhism: The pillars served as a medium to propagate Buddhist teachings and values.

• Architectural Impact: The Mauryan pillars influenced later Indian art and architecture, including the Gupta and post-Gupta periods.

• Conservation: Many of these pillars are now preserved in museums or are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

• Animal Capitals: The careful selection and carving of animal motifs highlight the skilled craftsmanship of the Mauryan artisans.

4. Which option best defines the musical form known as Svarajati?

A. A devotional song performed only in shrines

B. A dance piece without a fixed framework

C. A vocal composition later adapted into dance

D. A cinema-based fast-tempo form

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is A vocal composition later adapted into dance. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Svarajati is a unique and traditional musical form in Carnatic music, primarily used as a vocal composition. Over time, it has been adapted into dance performances, especially in Bharatanatyam.

• The composition typically comprises three sections: Pallavi, Anupallavi, and Charanam, which are essential for its structural framework and musical intricacy.

• Svarajatis are known for their expressive melodies and rhythmic patterns, making them suitable for conveying emotions and storytelling during dance performances.

• Historically, Svarajatis were composed to suit both vocal renditions and later evolved into an integral part of classical dance repertoires, highlighting their versatility.

• This form is distinct from other Carnatic compositions like Varnams and Krithis, as it serves as a bridge between pure music and dance expression.

• Characteristics of Svarajati: Svarajati compositions are renowned for their melodic richness and intricate rhythmic structures.

• They are often sung in slow tempo, allowing dancers to explore nuanced expressions and graceful movements.

• The lyrics of Svarajatis often revolve around devotion, mythology, or human emotions, making them relatable and impactful.

• Comparison with Other Forms: Unlike Varnams, which are more complex and elaborate, Svarajatis are relatively simpler in structure but equally expressive.

• Svarajatis differ from Krithis, as Krithis are primarily designed for vocal performance and devotion, whereas Svarajatis serve a dual purpose of music and dance.

• Historical Context: Svarajatis have been part of the Carnatic music tradition for centuries, with contributions from composers like Syama Sastri, who enriched this form.

• They were initially sung as standalone compositions but later gained prominence in Bharatanatyam dance performances.

• Applications: In dance, Svarajatis are used to depict stories, emotions, and devotional themes through a combination of music and movement.

• They are often performed in temple settings or during classical music and dance festivals, showcasing their cultural significance.

• Examples: Famous Svarajatis include those composed by Syama Sastri, such as "Kamakshi" in Bhairavi Raga, which is celebrated for its devotional depth.

• Modern interpretations of Svarajatis continue to be a part of Bharatanatyam performances, blending tradition with contemporary dance aesthetics.

• Option Analysis: Option 1: "A devotional song performed only in shrines" is incorrect, as Svarajati is not exclusively performed in shrines but is versatile for vocal and dance contexts.

• Option 2: "A dance piece without a fixed framework" is incorrect. Svarajatis have a structured framework consisting of Pallavi, Anupallavi, and Charanam.

• Option 3: "A vocal composition later adapted into dance" is correct, as Svarajatis were initially sung and later incorporated into Bharatanatyam dance.

• Option 4: "A cinema-based fast-tempo form" is incorrect, as Svarajatis are traditional compositions, not related to cinema or fast tempos.

11. Which limestone cave formation is correctly paired with its description?

A. Stalactite – Grows upward from floor

B. Stalagmite – Hangs from ceiling

C. Pillar – Created by joining stalactite and stalagmite

D. Stalagmite – Formed by water dripping from roof

Answer C

Solution

The correct answer is Pillar – Created by joining stalactite and stalagmite. Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• Pillar: A pillar in a limestone cave is formed when a stalactite (a formation hanging from the ceiling) and a stalagmite (a formation rising from the floor) grow towards each other and eventually meet. This process takes thousands of years due to the slow deposition of minerals like calcium carbonate.

• Stalactite: Incorrectly stated in the options, stalactites are formations hanging from the ceiling of caves. They are formed by the deposition of minerals from dripping water rich in calcium carbonate.

• Stalagmite: Incorrectly described in the options, stalagmites grow upward from the cave floor due to the dripping of mineral-laden water from the ceiling.

• Formation of a pillar is a slow process that involves both stalactites and stalagmites. These structures are often found in limestone caves, which provide the ideal environment for the deposition of minerals.

• The formation of pillars is a clear indication of the extensive geological activity within the cave and can serve as a natural timeline of mineral deposition.

• Stalactite: Derived from the Greek word "stalaktos," meaning "that which drips."

• They form when mineral-rich water drips from the ceiling of a cave. As the water evaporates, it leaves behind deposits of calcium carbonate, which gradually accumulate to form a stalactite.

• Stalactites are slender and often pointed at the tip, resembling icicles.

• Stalagmite: Derived from the Greek word "stalagmos," meaning "a drop or drip."

• They form when water that drips to the floor of the cave evaporates, leaving behind calcium carbonate deposits. These deposits build up over time to form stalagmites.

• Stalagmites are usually broader and more robust than stalactites and lack the pointed tip.

• Pillar Formation: Pillars, also known as columns, are formed when a stalactite and a stalagmite grow towards each other and merge into a single structure.

• The formation of a pillar requires stable environmental conditions, such as consistent mineral-rich water flow and a lack of disruptive geological activity.

• Pillars are significant geological features that help in studying the history and formation of caves.

• Limestone Caves: These caves are formed primarily by the dissolution of limestone due to acidic water. The water reacts with the calcium carbonate in the limestone, creating voids and caverns over time.

• Famous limestone caves include the Ajanta and Ellora Caves in India, the Carlsbad Caverns in the USA, and the Waitomo Caves in New Zealand.

• Speleology: The scientific study of caves and their formations is called speleology. It involves understanding the geological, biological, and ecological aspects of caves.

• Calcium Carbonate: The primary mineral involved in the formation of stalactites, stalagmites, and pillars. Its chemical formula is CaCO₃.

• Geological Time Scale: The formation of these structures occurs over thousands to millions of years, making them valuable records of Earth's geological history.

• Human Impact: Excessive tourism and environmental changes can disrupt the delicate balance required for cave formations to grow.

12. Which statement correctly describes the instruments onboard XPoSat?

A. XSPECT broadens POLIX polarimetry from 0.5–20 keV

B. POLIX performs timing and spectroscopy

C. XSPECT measures X-ray polarization

D. POLIX studies black holes via X-ray polarization (8–30 keV)

Answer D

Solution

The correct answer is POLIX studies black holes via X-ray polarization (8–30 keV). Key Points Additional Information Important Points

• XPoSat (X-ray Polarimeter Satellite) is India’s first dedicated polarimetry mission, developed by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO).

• It contains two primary instruments: POLIX (Polarimeter Instrument in X-rays) and XSPECT (X-ray Spectroscopy and Timing).

• POLIX studies X-ray polarization in the energy range of 8–30 keV, focusing on objects like black holes, neutron stars, and other celestial phenomena that emit X-rays. This statement is correct.

• XSPECT is designed for broad-band X-ray spectroscopy and timing, covering an energy range of 0.8–15 keV. It does not perform polarimetry. Hence, this is incorrect.

• The primary goal of XPoSat is to study the geometry and radiation mechanisms of various astronomical X-ray sources, with special emphasis on black holes and neutron stars.

• POLIX (Polarimeter Instrument in X-rays): POLIX is specifically designed to measure X-ray polarization, which helps understand the geometry and emission mechanisms of X-ray sources like black holes and neutron stars.

• It operates in the energy range of 8–30 keV, targeting high-energy cosmic phenomena.

• Polarization studies provide insights into the physical processes occurring in extreme environments, such as strong magnetic fields or intense gravitational fields.

• XSPECT (X-ray Spectroscopy and Timing): XSPECT focuses on X-ray spectroscopy and timing analysis in the 0.8–15 keV range.

• It studies the spectral properties and variability of X-ray sources but does not perform polarimetry.

• This instrument helps in understanding the chemical composition, temperature, and density of the emitting regions.

• Importance of X-ray Polarimetry: X-ray polarimetry is a unique tool to probe the geometry of astrophysical systems, such as accretion disks around black holes and neutron stars.

• By analyzing the degree and angle of polarization, scientists can infer details about magnetic field configurations and emission processes.

• POLIX’s capability is a significant step forward for Indian astrophysics and enhances our understanding of high-energy phenomena.

• Applications of XPoSat: Studying the polarization in X-rays emitted by cosmic sources.

• Investigating the magnetic field configurations and extreme physical conditions around compact objects.

• 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016: This act introduced the GST regime in India and is regarded as a landmark in indirect tax reform.

• It led to the insertion of new Articles like 246A, 269A, and 279A, and the deletion of Entry 92 and 92C from the Union List.

• Article 269A: This article deals with the apportionment of GST revenue on inter-state trade between the Centre and States.

• The Integrated GST (IGST) is levied on inter-state supplies, and its revenue is shared based on provisions in the GST Act.

• GST Structure: GST is levied at three levels: Central GST (CGST), State GST (SGST), and Integrated GST (IGST).

• CGST and SGST are levied on intra-state supplies, while IGST is applied to inter-state supplies.

• Cooperative Federalism in GST: GST represents a shift from a fragmented tax system to a unified tax structure in India.

• The GST Council exemplifies cooperative federalism, where both the Centre and States work together in framing GST rules and policies.

• Judicial Observations: In a landmark judgment (Union of India vs Mohit Minerals, 2022), the Supreme Court ruled that GST Council recommendations are non-binding and hold a persuasive value.

• This judgment reaffirmed the federal structure of GST and the discretion of legislatures in implementing its recommendations.
